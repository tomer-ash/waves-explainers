Impedance on a String

When a wave reaches the place where one string meets another, part of it bounces back and part goes on. One number per string decides how much: its impedance Z. This page builds that number from nothing, says what every letter is for, and lets you play with each idea.

The letters, and what each one is for

You will meet these on every page of lectures 07–10. Each has a job. The colour of a letter stays the same in every formula and figure below.

The string itself
\(\rho\) linear density kg/m

Mass per metre of string. It is the string's inertia: how hard it is to get it moving.

\(\Ten\) tension N

How hard the string is pulled. It is the restoring force: what pulls a bent string back straight.

\(v=\sqrt{\Ten/\rho}\) wave speed m/s

How fast a shape travels along the string. Tight and light means fast. (Called \(c\) in L05–06 and again from L09 p.9.)

\(\Zc=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\) impedance kg/s

How hard the string is to shake. Tight and heavy means high impedance. The only property of a string that a junction feels. Section 3.

The wave on it
\(\psi(x,t)\) displacement m

How far the point at position \(x\) is from the straight line, at time \(t\). The string's picture.

\(\dx\psi\) slope no unit

The steepness of the string at a point. Tension times slope is the vertical pull there.

\(\vy=\dt\psi\) transverse velocity m/s

How fast a point of the string moves up and down. Not the wave speed \(v\): the point goes up and down while the shape runs sideways.

\(f(t-x/v)\) a shape moving right

Any function of the single combination \(t-x/v\) is a right-mover; \(f(t+x/v)\) moves left. The lecture uses this to handle any pulse at once.

At a junction
\(\fI,\ \fR,\ \fT\) the three profiles

The incident shape coming in, the reflected one going back, the transmitted one going on. Their wave fields are \(\psiI,\psiR,\psiT\).

\(s\) the argument

Just a name for "whatever goes inside \(f\)". The lecture writes \(f(s)\) to stress it is one variable, not necessarily time (L07 p.3).

\(\Rc\) reflection coefficient no unit

Height of the echo ÷ height of the incoming pulse. Negative means the echo is upside down.

\(\Tc\) transmission coefficient no unit

Height of the pulse that goes on ÷ height of the incoming pulse. It can be bigger than 1.

Energy (L09)
\(\mathcal E\) energy density J/m

Energy stored per metre of string: motion (kinetic) plus stretch (potential).

\(P\) power W

Energy per second flowing past a point, counted positive to the right.

\(\RE,\ \TE\) power fractions

The share of the incoming power that goes back and that goes on. They add to 1. They are not \(\Rc\) and \(\Tc\).

One letter, three jobs. The lecture uses \(T\) for the period (L05), the tension (L06 on) and the transmission coefficient (L07 on), sometimes on the same page. Here the tension is always amber \(\Ten\) and the coefficient always green \(\Tc\). In the same way, \(\psi_t\) is the transmitted wave in L07 and L09 p.4, so this page writes the velocity as \(\vy\) or \(\dt\psi\), never \(\psi_t\).
1 · L07 p.1–3, L08 p.1

Why a junction needs exactly two rules

Until L07 the string was infinite and uniform, so nothing ever came back. What changes when two different strings are tied together?

Put string 1 on \(x<0\) and string 2 on \(x>0\), joined at \(x=0\). A pulse comes from the left. Two things can come out of the junction: an echo going left and a pulse going right. So there are two unknown shapes, \(\fR\) and \(\fT\). To find two unknowns you need two equations. The junction gives exactly two:

  1. The string does not tear. Both sides have the same height at \(x=0\) at every moment.
  2. No net force on the knot. The knot has no mass, so any net vertical force would give it infinite acceleration. The two vertical pulls must cancel.

The vertical pull of a string at a point is its tension times its slope (for small slopes). Try it:

With equal tensions the second rule says "the slope is continuous" (L07). When the tensions differ, it says \(\Teni1\,\dx\psi_L=\Teni2\,\dx\psi_R\) (L08 p.1). L08 builds such a junction from a massless ring sliding on a vertical rod: the rod takes up the sideways difference between the two tensions, so they can differ.

2 · L07 p.3–5, L08 p.2

From the two rules to R and T

What shapes come out, for any incoming pulse?

On the left the string carries the incoming and the reflected wave together; on the right only the transmitted one. Write each as a shape moving the right way, then apply the two rules at \(x=0\).

Derivation: reflection and transmission coefficients

  1. Write the field on each side. The incoming and transmitted shapes move right, the echo moves left (L07 p.1–2).
    \[\psi_L=\fI\!\left(t-\tfrac{x}{v_1}\right)+\fR\!\left(t+\tfrac{x}{v_1}\right),\qquad \psi_R=\fT\!\left(t-\tfrac{x}{v_2}\right)\]
  2. Rule 1, the string does not tear: set \(\psi_L=\psi_R\) at \(x=0\).
    \[\fI(t)+\fR(t)=\fT(t)\]
  3. Rule 2, no net force on the knot: \(\Teni1\dx\psi_L=\Teni2\dx\psi_R\) at \(x=0\). The chain rule gives \(\dx f(t\mp x/v)=\mp\tfrac1v f'\).
    \[-\frac{\Teni1}{v_1}\fI'+\frac{\Teni1}{v_1}\fR'=-\frac{\Teni2}{v_2}\fT'\]
  4. Every string enters only through the combination \(\Ten/v\). Give it a name: the impedance (L08 p.2). Then integrate in time; the constant is zero because the string is at rest before the pulse arrives.
    \[\Zc\equiv\frac{\Ten}{v}=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Zi1\bigl(\fI-\fR\bigr)=\Zi2\,\fT\]
  5. Two equations, two unknowns. Put \(\fT=\fI+\fR\) into step 4 and solve for \(\fR\), then for \(\fT\).
    \[\fR(s)=\frac{\Zi1-\Zi2}{\Zi1+\Zi2}\,\fI(s),\qquad \fT(s)=\frac{2\Zi1}{\Zi1+\Zi2}\,\fI(s)\]
\[\boxed{\ \Rc=\frac{\Zi1-\Zi2}{\Zi1+\Zi2},\qquad \Tc=\frac{2\Zi1}{\Zi1+\Zi2},\qquad 1+\Rc=\Tc\ }\]
Check: \(\Rc\) and \(\Tc\) have no units (a ratio of two impedances). Same string, \(\Zi1=\Zi2\): \(\Rc=0,\ \Tc=1\), as if there were no junction. Wall, \(\Zi2\to\infty\): \(\Rc=-1\). And \(1+\Rc=\Tc\) is just rule 1 divided by \(\fI\).

Why L07's formula looks different. L07 had one tension for both strings. Then \(\Zc=\Ten/v\) is proportional to \(1/v\), and the same result reads \(\Rc=\frac{v_2-v_1}{v_2+v_1}=\frac{\sqrt{\rho_1}-\sqrt{\rho_2}}{\sqrt{\rho_1}+\sqrt{\rho_2}}\). L08 p.2 gets the general form by replacing every \(v\) with \(1/\Zc\). The \(\Zc\) form is the one that always works.

What the shapes look like (L07 p.3–4)

Put the arguments back in and you get the whole picture, for any pulse shape:

\[\psiR(x,t)=\Rc\,\psiI(-x,t),\qquad \psiT(x,t)=\Tc\,\psiI\!\left(\tfrac{v_1}{v_2}x,\ t\right)\]

The mirror trick. \(\psiI(-x,t)\) is the incoming pulse as if the junction were not there (it would have carried on into \(x>0\)), reflected in a mirror at \(x=0\). The echo is that mirror image times \(\Rc\). Tick "show the mirror trick" in the top figure: the grey dashed pulse is the one that "would have continued", the grey dotted one is its mirror image, and the red echo is the dotted one scaled by \(\Rc\).

The transmitted pulse has the same shape and the same duration in time, scaled by \(\Tc\). Because it moves \(v_2/v_1\) times faster, it is \(v_2/v_1\) times wider in space: "similar, but faster and wider" (L07 p.4).

What impedance actually is

The left string only ever touches the knot. So what does the whole right-hand string feel like, seen from the knot?

Answer: like a damper, a piston in oil that pushes back in proportion to how fast you move it. Shake the end of a long string, and the force you feel is exactly what a damper of constant \(\Zc\) would give. Drag the black handle up and down (or watch it move by itself):

Pink arrow: the velocity \(\vy\) of the hand. Violet arrow: the force pushing back on the hand. The string's force is computed from the string's own slope at the end (\(\Ten\,\dx\psi\)); the damper's from \(-\Zc\vy\). On wider screens two force traces appear on the right, and they lie on top of each other. Raise the tension or the density and the push-back grows. (Real numbers; the string is shown in slow motion, and a full lane height is 2 cm of real displacement.)

Derivation: the string beyond the knot is a damper (L08 p.2–3)

  1. The vertical force of string 2 on the knot is its tension times its slope there.
    \[F_y=\Teni2\,\dx\psi_R\big|_{x=0}\]
  2. String 2 carries only a wave moving away, \(\fT(t-x/v_2)\). For such a wave the slope is fixed by the velocity: \(\dx\fT=-\tfrac1{v_2}\dt\fT\).
    \[F_y=-\frac{\Teni2}{v_2}\,\dt\psi_R\big|_{x=0}\]
  3. \(\Teni2/v_2\) is \(\Zi2\), and \(\dt\psi\) at the knot is its velocity \(\vy\).
    \[\boxed{\ F_y=-\Zc\,\vy\ }\]
Check: units \(\mathrm{N}/(\mathrm{m/s})=\mathrm{kg/s}\) ✓. The minus sign: the force always opposes the motion, like friction. "A large impedance means strong damping" (L08 p.3). Condition: step 2 needs a wave moving away only. If something comes back (a second junction, a wall far away), the string is no longer a pure damper; that is how standing waves arise in section 6.

That is the purpose of \(\Zc\): it replaces the whole string beyond a point by one number, the ratio of force to velocity. That is also why the junction "sees" only \(\Zi1\) and \(\Zi2\), and why two strings with the same \(\Zc\) look identical to it, whatever their \(\rho\) and \(\Ten\) separately (L08 p.3–4).

Why \(\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\)? mine, a picture Move the end up at speed \(\vy\). In one second the wave front runs a distance \(v\) along the string, so a length \(v\) of string, of mass \(\rho v\), goes from rest to speed \(\vy\). The momentum you hand out per second is \(\rho v\cdot\vy\), and momentum per second is force: \(F=\rho v\,\vy\). So \(\Zc=\rho v=\rho\sqrt{\Ten/\rho}=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\). Heavier string: more mass to set moving. Tighter string: the front runs faster and recruits more string each second. Both raise \(\Zc\).

Where does the energy go? A real damper turns your work into heat. The string does not: it carries the energy away as a wave, and a load far away can collect it (Crawford 4.4). That is why "impedance" and not "friction": the string resists like a damper, but it passes the energy on.

Matching: when the junction disappears

When does everything pass through? And is a "softer" second string better?

\(\Rc=0\) exactly when \(\Zi1=\Zi2\). This is impedance matching. The second string then pushes back on the knot exactly as the first string itself would, so the knot cannot tell that anything changed. Drag along the curve:

The trap the lecturer warns about (L08 p.4)

"One might think transmission would be better for a low impedance, as if there were less resistance. זה לא נכון!" Go left on the curve. \(\Tc\) climbs towards 2, so the transmitted pulse is taller. But the power through, \(\TE\), falls to zero. Why: a low-impedance string barely pushes back on the knot (\(F_y=-\Zi2\vy\) is tiny), and power is force times velocity. A big wiggle with no force behind it carries almost nothing. At \(\Zi2=0\) the end is free: it swings to twice the height and delivers no power at all.

Same impedance, different speed

Matching is about \(\Zc\), not about the speed. The lecture's example (L08 p.3): \(\Teni2=3\Teni1\) and \(\rho_2=\rho_1/3\). Then \(\Zi2=\sqrt{3\Teni1\cdot\rho_1/3}=\Zi1\), matched, while \(v_2=\sqrt{3\Teni1/(\rho_1/3)}=3v_1\).

The same rule in a circuit (L08 p.4–5)

Read \(\Zc=F/v\) with force → voltage \(V\) and velocity → current \(I\): it becomes \(R=V/I\), Ohm's law. A source with internal resistance \(R_S\) feeds a load \(R_L\). The load receives \(P_L=V^2R_L/(R_S+R_L)^2\), and setting \(\partial P_L/\partial R_L=0\) gives \(R_L=R_S\): the load must match the source.

Complex impedance (L08 p.6). Capacitors and coils make the "resistance" complex; it is then called impedance, and its complex part "simply describes a phase difference between the source and the load". Matching still decides how much power gets through, which is why it matters for transmission lines and antennas.

share of the maximum power

mine, a connection Divided by its maximum \(V^2/4R_S\), the load power is \(\dfrac{4R_SR_L}{(R_S+R_L)^2}\). That is exactly \(\TE=\dfrac{4\Zi1\Zi2}{(\Zi1+\Zi2)^2}\) of the strings, the dashed green curve above. Same curve, same peak at "equal".

The megaphone (מגהפון, L08 p.6): change slowly

Sometimes you cannot pick \(\Zi2\); the two media are given (a loudspeaker and the room, a probe and skin). The fix: join them through a region whose impedance changes gradually, slowly compared with the wavelength. Each small step reflects a tiny wave, and over a long taper these tiny echoes cancel each other. Compare an abrupt jump with a gradual one of the same total size:

Energy: impedance is also the power price

A wave carries energy along. How much power, and how is it shared at a junction?

A small piece \(dx\) of string has two kinds of energy. It moves, so it has kinetic energy \(\tfrac12(\rho\,dx)\vy^2\). It is bent, so it is stretched a little, by \(\tfrac12(\dx\psi)^2dx\); that costs \(\Ten\) times the stretch, potential energy \(\tfrac12\Ten(\dx\psi)^2dx\) (L09 p.1–2). Per metre:

\[\mathcal E=\tfrac12\rho\,(\dt\psi)^2+\tfrac12\Ten\,(\dx\psi)^2\]

Derivation: power carried by a wave (L09 p.3–4)

  1. Power is force times velocity. The string on the left of a point pulls the string on its right with the vertical force \(-\Ten\dx\psi\), and that point moves at \(\vy=\dt\psi\).
    \[P=-\Ten\,\dx\psi\,\dt\psi\]
  2. For a wave moving right, the slope is fixed by the velocity, \(\dx\psi=-\tfrac1v\dt\psi\) (the same step as in section 3).
    \[P=\frac{\Ten}{v}(\dt\psi)^2\]
  3. \(\Ten/v=\Zc\) again. The power is the impedance times the velocity squared. The same step shows kinetic = potential, so \(\mathcal E=\rho(\dt\psi)^2\) and \(P=v\,\mathcal E\).
    \[\boxed{\ P=\Zc\,(\dt\psi)^2=v\,\mathcal E\ }\quad\text{(right-mover; a left-mover has }P=-\Zc(\dt\psi)^2)\]
  4. At the junction, all three waves have the same time profile at \(x=0\), scaled by 1, \(\Rc\), \(\Tc\). The echo moves in string 1 (\(\Zi1\)), the transmitted wave in string 2 (\(\Zi2\)). Compare their powers with the incoming one (L09 p.4–5).
    \[\RE=\left|\frac{P_r}{P_i}\right|=\frac{\Zi1\Rc^2}{\Zi1}=\Rc^2,\qquad \TE=\left|\frac{P_t}{P_i}\right|=\frac{\Zi2}{\Zi1}\Tc^2=\frac{4\Zi1\Zi2}{(\Zi1+\Zi2)^2}\]
\[\boxed{\ \RE=\Rc^2,\qquad \TE=\frac{\Zi2}{\Zi1}\,\Tc^2,\qquad \RE+\TE=\frac{(\Zi1-\Zi2)^2+4\Zi1\Zi2}{(\Zi1+\Zi2)^2}=1\ }\]
Check: units of \(\Zc(\dt\psi)^2\) are \(\mathrm{kg/s}\cdot\mathrm{m^2/s^2}=\mathrm{W}\) ✓. Energy is conserved at the junction ✓. Trap: \(\Tc^2\) alone is not the power fraction. \(\Tc\) compares heights; power also needs the impedance of the string it travels on, hence the factor \(\Zi2/\Zi1\).

So \(\Zc\) has a second job: it converts "how fast the string moves" into "how much power flows". The same string motion carries more power on a high-impedance string. The figure shows where the energy sits. Switch between a travelling and a standing wave:

Standing waves are impedance at the extremes

What happens when the wave keeps coming and the end reflects all of it?

L10 starts with a sentence that ties everything together: "the wall behaves like an impedance on the left that is infinite: full reflection." A wall is \(\Zc=\infty\), so \(\Rc=-1\); a free end is \(\Zc=0\), so \(\Rc=+1\). Send in a steady wave instead of one pulse, and the incoming wave and its echo overlap everywhere. Their sum is a standing wave: a shape that grows and shrinks in place, with points that never move (nodes, צמתים).

Here the picture follows L10: the wall is on the left at \(x=0\), the string on \(x>0\), and the wave comes in from the right, \(\psiI=A\cos(\omega t+kx+\phi)\). Slide the impedance of the end and watch the dips:

Derivation: the standing wave at a wall (L10 p.1)

  1. The wall reflects with \(\Rc=-1\), and the echo is \(\Rc\,\psiI(-x,t)\) (section 2).
    \[\psiR=-A\cos(\omega t-kx+\phi)\]
  2. Add them, using \(\cos a-\cos b=-2\sin\tfrac{a+b}2\sin\tfrac{a-b}2\).
    \[\psi=A\cos(\omega t+kx+\phi)-A\cos(\omega t-kx+\phi)=-2A\sin(\omega t+\phi)\,\sin kx\]
  3. Time and space have separated: \(\sin kx\) fixes the shape, \(\sin(\omega t+\phi)\) only scales it. Points with \(\sin kx=0\) never move.
    \[\boxed{\ \text{nodes at } kx=n\pi,\ \text{spaced } \lambda/2\ }\]
Check: \(\psi(0,t)=0\) for all \(t\), as a wall demands ✓. For a free end (\(\Rc=+1\)) the same steps give \(2A\cos(\omega t+\phi)\cos kx\): an antinode at the end, zero slope there (L10 p.2–3).

Two walls: only some wavelengths fit (L10 p.3–4)

A string of length \(L\) tied at both ends needs a node at \(x=L\) as well: \(\sin kL=0\). So \(k\) can no longer be anything:

\[k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\qquad \lambda_n=\frac{2L}{n},\qquad \nu_n=\frac{n\,v}{2L},\qquad \psi(x,t)=\sum_n B_n\sin(\omega_nt+\phi_n)\sin k_nx\]

These are the normal modes. Every frequency is a whole multiple of the lowest one, \(\nu_1=v/2L\). Any motion of the string is a sum of modes, with \(B_n,\phi_n\) fixed by the starting shape and velocity.

No net power in a standing wave (L10 p.5). With \(\psi=A\sin\omega t\sin kx\), the power \(P=-\Ten\dx\psi\dt\psi\) contains \(\sin\omega t\cos\omega t=\tfrac12\sin2\omega t\), which averages to zero over a period. At any instant energy moves, but it only sloshes back and forth between neighbouring nodes. Switch the energy figure in section 5 to "standing" to see it.

Wave packets at a junction

A real pulse is made of many frequencies. Why does it keep its shape when it reflects?

A wave packet is a sum of many single-frequency waves. Add two and you get beats: a fast carrier inside a slow envelope. Add many, with weights that fall off smoothly around a central wave number \(k_0\), and all the bumps but one cancel: a single packet, a carrier \(\cos k_0x\) inside an envelope \(h(x)\) (L12 writes \(\psi(x,0)=h(x)e^{ik_0x}\)). Build one:

Now the link to impedance. At a junction, each single-frequency part of the packet reflects with its own \(\Rc\). On a string \(\Zc=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\) contains no frequency at all, so every part gets the same \(\Rc\) and the same \(\Tc\). The echo is therefore the whole packet, mirrored and scaled, and the transmitted packet is the whole packet, scaled and stretched by \(v_2/v_1\). That is exactly why L07 could write \(\psiR=\Rc\,\psiI(-x,t)\) for any shape. On a string every part also travels at the same speed \(v\) (\(\omega=vk\)), so the packet does not spread out; packets that do spread need dispersion (L11–L12).

Press "Send a packet into the junction", or choose "Wave packet" in the top figure: the carrier wiggles inside the echo flip together when \(\Rc<0\), and the transmitted packet keeps the same number of wiggles while it gets wider or narrower.

8 · L08 p.4–6, recitation 5, Crawford 4.4

One idea, many media

Is impedance only about strings?

No. Every wave has a "push" quantity and a "motion" quantity, and \(\Zc\) is their ratio. Once you know a medium's \(\Zc\), every result on this page applies unchanged.

The pattern in the impedance column: always \(\sqrt{\text{stiffness}\times\text{inertia}}\). For the cable, \(L\) plays the inertia (like \(\rho\)) and \(1/C\) the stiffness (like \(\Ten\)): \(\sqrt{L\cdot(1/C)}=\sqrt{L/C}\). The speed is the other combination, \(\sqrt{\text{stiffness}/\text{inertia}}\): \(v=\sqrt{\Ten/\rho}\) and \(c=1/\sqrt{LC}\).

A cable at a short circuit (\(Z_2=0\)) is the free end of the string: the current (velocity-like) doubles, while the voltage (force-like) reflects with \(-\Rc=-1\) and is zero at the short. No voltage, no power delivered. This is the circuit version of the trap in section 4.

Check yourself

No numbers, just the ideas. Answer in your head, then open.

A pulse goes from a light string to a heavy one with the same tension. Is the echo upright or upside down?
Upside down. Heavy means larger \(\Zc=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\), so \(\Zi2>\Zi1\) and \(\Rc=\frac{\Zi1-\Zi2}{\Zi1+\Zi2}<0\).
\(\Tc\) can be bigger than 1. Doesn't that break energy conservation?
No. \(\Tc\) compares heights, not energy. The power fraction is \(\TE=\frac{\Zi2}{\Zi1}\Tc^2\le1\). A tall pulse on a low-impedance string carries little power.
Can two strings with different wave speeds be joined without any echo?
Yes, if their impedances are equal. Example: three times the tension and a third of the density gives the same \(\Zc\) but three times the speed. The junction feels only \(\Zc\).
Why doesn't a lower \(\Zi2\) pass more power, "since it resists less"?
Power is force times velocity, and the force on the knot is \(\Zi2\vy\). A low-impedance string barely pushes, so little power goes in; at \(\Zi2=0\) none. Maximum power through is at \(\Zi2=\Zi1\).
What is a wall, in impedance language? And a free end?
Wall: \(\Zc=\infty\), \(\Rc=-1\), a node (no motion) at the wall. Free end: \(\Zc=0\), \(\Rc=+1\), an antinode, with zero slope at the end.
Doubling the tension of a string (same density) changes its impedance and its speed by what?
Both by \(\sqrt2\): \(\Zc=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}\) and \(v=\sqrt{\Ten/\rho}\).
"The rest of the string is a damper with \(F_y=-\Zc\vy\)." When is this false?
When something comes back along the string: a second junction or a fixed end beyond. The derivation used a wave moving only away. With waves both ways (a standing wave) the force is no longer proportional to the velocity.
If the string acts like a damper, why doesn't it heat up?
It doesn't dissipate the energy, it carries it away as a wave. A damper turns the work into heat; the string passes it on to whatever is further along.
What is the time-averaged power in a standing wave, and why?
Zero. It is two equal waves going opposite ways, so their powers cancel; directly, \(P\propto\sin\omega t\cos\omega t\), which averages to zero. Energy sloshes between nodes but never crosses one.
A wave packet hits a string junction. Does its shape change?
Only by scaling (and mirroring for the echo). The transmitted packet is stretched in space by \(v_2/v_1\) but lasts the same time. On a string \(\Zc\) has no frequency in it, so every frequency in the packet gets the same \(\Rc\) and \(\Tc\).
What does ultrasound gel do?
It removes the air between the probe and the skin. Air has a very different impedance from both, so it would reflect almost everything. With the gel the step is small and most of the sound goes in (L08 p.6).
A wave hits a junction. Which stays the same on both sides: frequency, wavelength or speed?
The frequency. The knot moves at one frequency, and it drives string 2 at that frequency. The speed changes to \(v_2\), so the wavelength changes to \(v_2/\nu\).

One-screen recap

Each formula with the sentence that goes with it.

\[\Zc=\frac{\Ten}{v}=\sqrt{\Ten\rho}=\rho v\]

How hard a string is to shake. Grows with tension and with density.

\[F_y=-\Zc\,\vy\]

The string beyond a point acts like a damper of constant \(\Zc\), while nothing comes back.

\[\Rc=\frac{\Zi1-\Zi2}{\Zi1+\Zi2},\quad \Tc=\frac{2\Zi1}{\Zi1+\Zi2}\]

Heights of the echo and of the pulse that goes on. \(1+\Rc=\Tc\).

\[\psiR=\Rc\,\psiI(-x,t),\quad \psiT=\Tc\,\psiI\!\left(\tfrac{v_1}{v_2}x,t\right)\]

Any shape: the echo is the mirror image; the pulse that goes on is wider by \(v_2/v_1\).

\[P=\Zc(\dt\psi)^2=v\,\mathcal E\]

Power of a right-moving wave. Kinetic = potential energy.

\[\RE=\Rc^2,\quad \TE=\frac{\Zi2}{\Zi1}\Tc^2,\quad \RE+\TE=1\]

Shares of the power. \(\Tc^2\) alone is not the power share.

\[\Zi1=\Zi2\ \Rightarrow\ \Rc=0\]

Matching: the junction disappears. Lower \(\Zi2\) is not better.

\[\text{wall } \Zc=\infty:\ \Rc=-1;\quad \text{free } \Zc=0:\ \Rc=+1\]

Total reflection. With a steady wave: a standing wave, nodes every \(\lambda/2\), no net power.

\[k_n=\frac{n\pi}{L},\quad \nu_n=\frac{nv}{2L}\]

Two fixed ends allow only whole numbers of half wavelengths.
